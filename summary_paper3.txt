Abstract— The internal temperatures and the ability to dissi-
pate the heat from active part influence the windings’ insulation
deterioration and determine how long power transformers are
able to remain in operation. Due to the great importance
of this equipment for the electrical system, studies to avoid
failures and early aging are needed. This article presents an
evaluation of thermal performance of power transformers and the
optical fiber sensor positioning to temperature monitoring. For
this, an experimental and numerical methodology is performed,
comparing the measurements during the temperature rise test
of single-phase prototype power transformer, under oil directed
and air natural (ODAN) cooling system conditions equipped with
20 optical fiber sensors along its windings, to the obtained results
of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations performed
in ANSYS Fluent software. It was verified that the punctual
temperature measured by optical sensors may not accurately
represent the critical temperatures along transformers’ windings,
and the windings’ arrangements impact on oil flow and tempera-
ture distribution, as the number and positioning of block washers
being necessary to be considered in previous studies for a better
positioning of the sensors and temperature monitoring.
Index Terms— Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simula-
tion, optical fiber sensor, power transformer, temperature rise
test, thermal performance.

Currently, two techniques are commonly used during the

Nevertheless, the use and development of both techniques are
essential to develop optimal designs for power transformers.

risk of bubble formation, and partial discharges.

II. THERMAL STUDY OF POWER TRANSFORMERS
A. Heating and Heat Transfer Modes
Once power transformer is in operation, the heat generated
by the windings and the core increases the oil temperature.
In this equipment, three modes of heat transfer are present:
conduction, convection, and radiation. The heat generated by
the windings is transferred to the fluid due to convection. The
thermosiphon cooling is what coordinates the heat transfer and
the oil flow in smaller transformers, which do not need a high
rate of heat transfer and natural convection is enough [5].
However, in larger transformers which work with high
power processing, a pump is installed to increase the flow of
fluid inside the winding, thus increasing the heat dissipation
rate. Nevertheless, the oil flow is governed by the least
resistance path and may not go equally through the windings’
channels. To solve this issue, large rating transformers have
predetermined paths that direct the oil to the windings and
core, hence increasing effectiveness of the cooling system [5].
The use of washers also improves the heat transfer at the
windings by distributing fluid flow through radial and axial
channels.
B. Cooling Systems
Liquid-immersed transformers are classified according to
their cooling method, by a four-letter code, which describes

the cooling mediums of the transformers and their circulation
mechanisms [23]. The oil can circulate through windings
in three different modes, having different effects during the
heating process. The natural-oil (ON) mode consists in the
circulation by fluid natural convection. Using a hydraulic
pump, this circulation can be by forced-oil (OF), when the
pump forces the oil flow to inside the tank, or directed-oil
(OD), with the oil flow forced by the pump and directed to
inside the windings by structures at the bottom of the active
part. In general, ON mode has a worse thermal performance
in relation to OF and OD ones; however, it is more reliable
and economic, once it do not use pumps. Other important
characteristic of the internal cooling modes is the gradient
temperature between the bottom and top of the windings. The
OD mode results in a small variation, while ON and OF modes
result in bigger ones, which must be considered to analyze
the equipment’s lifespan [5], [14]. In relation to the external
cooling, the air can also circulate in a natural mode (AN),
due to its natural convection process, or with the help of
fans coupled to radiators to force the air circulation (AF) and
increase the thermal transfer. Thus, a tradeoff between costs
and thermal performance is observed, once the use of pumps
and fans represents more costs with acquisition and monitoring
of these equipment and this also leads to a better thermal
performance [5], [14]. In this way, a thermal analysis must
be done to decide the better cooling system configuration to
each application. This article analyzes an ODAN transformer;
thus, the oil is pumped through predetermined paths along
the windings, and the oil transfers its heat to outside ambient
without any forced air flow.
C. Temperatures in Power Transformers
During operation, power transformers are constantly suffer-
ing thermal stress. The degradation process of oil impregnated
kraft papers can be related to the temperature through the
Arrhenius equations, whereas the temperature rise beyond the
normal operating conditions of the equipment results in an
exponential acceleration of its aging. Thus, a limit of 65 ◦C
for the top oil temperature rising is stipulated, in order to avoid
the transformer’s early aging [24], [25].
Therefore, the monitoring of internal temperatures along
windings, focusing in top oil and hotspot, is essential for
maintaining the integrity and increasing the transformer life
span [22]. PT100 sensors [26] and thermocouple sensors are
used to measure temperatures along the windings [16], [17],
[27]. However, due to their characteristics, optical fiber sensors
are allocated along windings and provide a reliable way to
collect the data to monitor and predict the thermal performance
and life expectancy of the transformer [22]. On the other hand,
optical sensors are too vulnerable to mechanical damages,
so the transformer production with these technology needs to
follow some guidelines to ensure the correct operation [21],
[22]. In general, the sensor is centrally positioned along
the radial channel between two disks, facing the upper one,
inserted in a paper spacer, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

temperature of the local where it is fixed. The goal is

III. ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY
This article uses an experimental and computational coupled
methodology in order to analyze power transformer thermal
performance and to evaluate the optical sensor positioning.
The methodology diagram is illustrated in Fig. 2. Temperature
rise test is performed on a transformer prototype and, using
a complete experimental setup and a temperature measure-
ment system based on optical fiber sensors, the temperature
distribution reached at steady state along the windings is
obtained. In parallel, CFD simulations are performed, with
preprocessing, processing, and post-processing stages.
During the preprocessing, the transformer constructive
dimensions and materials from the experimental test are the
input data for the modeling step. Once the geometry is
properly modeled, the mesh step is performed. In sequence,
the simulation setup is configured with input data from the heat
run test, such as the windings’ thermal losses, the pumped oil
flow, and the ambient temperature. The simulations are solved
during the processing stage, and the complete temperature
distribution along windings is obtained and analyzed at the
post-processing, when they are finalized.
To ensure the quality of the results, a grid-dependent
study is done, refining the mesh after each simulation until
controlling parameters do not vary or present a negligible
variation. At this point, the temperature distributions obtained
from experimental and computational analysis are compared,
analyzed, and validated. The experimental and computational
details are explained in Sections IV and V.

V. COMPUTATIONAL FLUID DYNAMICS
In order to complement the thermal analysis provided by the
heat run test, CFD simulations were performed. This section
details the CFD methodology used to compute the flow and
temperature distributions in power transformer, following the
steps in Fig. 2. This technique solves the governing equations
of fluid flow and heat transfer, state mass, momentum, and
energy equations.
A. Modeling
As shown in Fig. 2, a precise model of the transformer
prototype HV windings is built, receiving the constructive
dimensions and characteristics, presented in Figs. 4 and 5,
and materials data from the experiment. To a time processing
reduction, only the oil domain was modeled, where the heat
transfer properly happens and the results need to be evaluated.

B. Meshing
The modeled windings are divided into finite volumes,
forming a mesh. In order to eliminate errors due to a
low-quality mesh and to ensure reliable results, a grid
dependence study is made. It consists of a procedure of
successive refinement of an initial coarse grid until certain
controlling parameters do not change, making the simula-
tion grid-independent. Thus, a systematic search for grid-
independent results forms an essential part of all high-quality
CFD studies [32].
In this way, a grid-refinement study was performed in this
work. An initial mesh, already with good quality elements,
and its temperature distribution at the Column 1 entrance are
illustrated in Fig. 11, while Fig. 12 shows the final mesh
and its temperature distribution. It is possible to observe the
convergence of the results in face of mesh refinement.
Optimal meshes are often nonuniform: finer in areas where
large variations occur from point to point and coarser in
regions with relatively little change [32]. Thus, a refinement
was done near the faces of the disks, in order to evaluate more
precisely the temperatures at these regions.

VI. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As shown in Fig. 2, with the experiment and simulations
done, the obtained temperature distributions are analyzed.
From CFD simulations, the temperature contours along HV
windings are illustrated in 2-D cuts in Figs. 13 and 14, for
Columns 1 and 2, respectively. To facilitate the visualization,
the windings’ plots were partitioned in four parts, each one
with 15 disks. In general, the oil temperature increases from
bottom to top of windings, entering with 329 K (56 ◦C) and
exiting about 335 K (62 ◦C). This small difference when
compared to the absolute values of the entire windings is
expected, due to the characteristic of OD mode, as mentioned
in Section II-B. Also, the washers’ positioning is observed,
where the oil cannot flow, and some differences, as a hotspot
in the first radial channels in Column 1, are not observed in
Column 2.
In order to investigate the hottest point of Column 1,
Figs. 15 and 16 show with detail the temperature and flow
vector distributions, respectively, at the two first radial chan-
nels from Columns 1 and 2. It is noted that the constructive
difference between the columns leads to different oil flow and
temperature fields. The washer presence at the entrance of
external axial channel in Column 2 forces the oil to enter
totally through internal axial channel and, due the hydraulic
resistances of winding, results in a more intense flow at the
initial radial channels [see Fig. 16(b)]. On the other hand,
Column 1 does not have washer at the inlet; then the oil
enters divided through internal and external axial channels.
Consequently, the oil flow at the initial radial channels is too
low, almost stagnated, reducing the heat transfer ratio and
increasing the local temperature [see Fig. 16(a)].
In addition, Fig. 15 shows that the hottest temperature is
not exactly at the middle of the radial channel and its location
is governed by the dynamic of the oil flow. Complementing,

VII. CONCLUSION
In order to understand the thermal performance of power
transformers and to contribute to better optical sensor posi-
tioning designs, this article presented a thermal analysis of HV
windings under ODAN conditions through CFD simulation.
The computed data were compared through a temperature rise
test results of a prototype transformer equipped with 20 optical
sensors along its windings. The simulations showed good
accuracy in determining the prototype transformer tempera-
tures, presenting low deviation, and validating the employed
methodology.
The CFD simulation allowed to observe details that are not
detected by the optical instrumentation, being a great tool
to transformer design. It was observed that the number and
positioning of washers influence the oil flow distribution and
orientation at the radial channels, which, in turn, influences
the temperature distribution along the windings. In addition,
the temperature distribution shows that the absence of block

In relation to the differences found between simulations and
experimental results, it could be explained by some reasons.
One of them is the uniform distribution of thermal losses
applied along the windings, which is known that in practice
it could be different. Also, the transformer manufacturing
process has uncertainties and restrictions, which cannot be
predicted and reproduced by the CFD model. Therefore, once
validated, this CFD methodology can be used to analyze
constructive and material variations during design stages,
including the distribution and positioning of block washers
and optical sensors, looking for an optimal design of windings,
and contributing for transformer industry. Finally, this study
will be extended to other cooling systems, as OF and ON,
to investigate the related issues, through CFD simulations and
heat run tests.
